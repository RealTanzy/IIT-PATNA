When Should Language Models
Search? A Topology-Aware Analysis
of Reasoning Strategies
 (/pdf?
id=EKdClSRoGE)
Dibyanayan Bandyopadhyay (/profile?id=~Dibyanayan_Bandyopadhyay1),
MD Tanzeel Adam Khan (/profile?id=~MD_Tanzeel_Adam_Khan1),
Asif Ekbal (/profile?id=~Asif_Ekbal1)
Keywords: applications, chain-of-thought
Abstract:
Search-based reasoning and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting offer two contrasting inference-time paradigms for language
models: CoT commits to a single linear trajectory, while search explores alternative partial reasoning paths before selecting an
answer. In this work, we study this trade-off using A* as a controlled and optimal instantiation of heuristic-guided search.
Across StrategyQA, LogicQA, HotpotQA, GSM8K, and MATH, and across three model scales, we find that search is not a
universal upgrade over CoT; instead, the two strategies solve substantially different subsets of instances. To explain this
complementarity, we introduce a \emph{Reasoning Topology Framework} based on structural features computable before
generation. A derived \emph{branching coefficient} predicts when search helps better than dataset identity, indicating that
the CoT--search boundary is primarily topology-dependent. We further train a topology-aware router that selects between CoT
and A* without observing generated traces. Despite this restriction, it outperforms post-hoc routers based on semantic
entropy and LLM-as-critic judgments. We attribute this to a \emph{Fluency--Correctness Asymmetry}: fluent traces are often
incorrect, making trace-level adjudication unreliable. Overall, our results suggest that adaptive reasoning should route by
problem structure, deploying search only when the input topology warrants its higher cost. Code:

Paper Summary:
The authors use A* search-based reasoning to investigate how LLms reason, whether it be through search-based or
single trajectory reasoning. When investigating using several reasoning benchmarks like StrategyQA, LogicQA,
HotpotQA, GSM8K, and MATH, they find that search is not always the optimal reasoning methodology, as it depends
on the type of reasoning and specific task. As a result, they introduce a reasoning topology framework to determine
which reasoning strategy is most effective depending on the context and question.

Compute analysis conducted by the authors is an important part of the paper, as the authors acknowledge that
A* is substantially more expensive than CoT.
The interpretability of the proposed topology framework is the main contribution of this work, and shows how
and when search can help, particularly in highly branched reasoning contexts.

Comment:
Based on my understanding of the paper, A* uses self-reported confidence. The
authors should analyze this part of the pipeline further, to identify and
understand potential drawbacks.
Response: Thank you for highlighting this point. The A* procedure uses self-reported confidence only in
the accumulated path cost, 
, rather than in the heuristic itself. The heuristic is a
deterministic progress critic based on reasoning depth and question-content coverage. As stated in the
manuscript, we treat 
 as a bounded search-ranking signal, not as a calibrated probability: each transition
always incurs a base cost of 1, while confidence contributes only an additional penalty between 0 and 1.
This design limits, but does not eliminate, the drawbacks of self-reported confidence. An incorrect but
overconfident step may be prioritized and lead to premature commitment, whereas a correct but
underconfident step may be explored later. Our failure analysis captures these behaviors: among A*
failures on instances correctly solved by CoT, 8.9% result from premature commitment, while 91.1% result
from overthinking, where search continues beyond sufficient evidence and accumulates distractors. Thus,
the dominant observed limitation is excessive exploration rather than confidence alone. Moreover, the
independent depth–coverage heuristic and voting over multiple goal traces reduce dependence on any
single confidence estimate. Accordingly, our claims concern this controlled A* instantiation and do not
assume that self-reported confidence is calibrated or universally reliable.
W2. The human validation of the reasoning-trace quality shows a moderate
agreement between moderators on a relatively small sample (100). How does
this validation support the authors' work?
Response: Thank you for raising this question. The human evaluation contains 100 CoT–A* disagreement
instances from each of StrategyQA and LogicQA, for 200 annotated instances in total, selected from
disagreement pools of 587 and 332 instances, respectively. The purpose of this study is not to establish
aggregate performance, which is supported by the full-benchmark accuracy and instance-level
decomposition, but to diagnose why the two strategies succeed on different examples.
The results show the same pattern across both datasets and in both directions. When CoT is correct and A*
is wrong, CoT has substantially higher reasoning coverage than A*: 3.00 versus 1.20 on StrategyQA and
3.00 versus 1.86 on LogicQA. Conversely, when A* is correct, A* has higher coverage: 2.71 versus 1.63 on
StrategyQA and 2.77 versus 1.88 on LogicQA. Relevance is much less strongly associated with correctness,
showing that a trace can remain on-topic while still missing the decisive inferential steps.
We interpret the moderate agreement, 
–
, as reflecting the inherent subjectivity of ordinal
trace-quality assessment, and therefore do not use the human scores as primary statistical evidence.
Rather, their consistent directional pattern supports the narrower mechanistic conclusion that CoT–A*
complementarity is driven mainly by whether the strategy covers the required reasoning path, rather than
by superficial fluency or stylistic quality.

Paper Summary:
This paper analyzes when language models should use Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning and when they should use
search-based reasoning during inference. The authors use A* search as a controlled search method and compare it
with CoT, showing that neither strategy uniformly outperforms the other; instead, they are complementary and solve
different types of problems. To explain this complementarity, the paper introduces reasoning topology features and a
branching coefficient that characterize the structural properties of each problem. Based on these features, the
authors train a topology-aware router that selects between CoT and A* before generation. The experimental results
suggest that search is more beneficial for multi-hop or branch-heavy problems, whereas CoT can be more suitable for
simpler, more linear problems.
Summary Of Strengths:
1. The research question is clear and practical. Rather than simply asking whether search is better than CoT, the
paper addresses the more useful question of when search is actually needed. This is an important issue for
inference-time reasoning, where both accuracy and computational cost must be considered.
2. The paper analyzes the complementarity between CoT and search at the instance level. Across multiple
benchmarks and model scales, the authors show that CoT and A* solve different subsets of problems, and the
oracle gap makes a strong case for routing between reasoning strategies. This goes beyond average accuracy
comparisons and helps identify which types of problems benefit from each strategy.
3. The paper proposes an interpretable and interesting direction through topology-aware routing. Instead of
evaluating reasoning traces after generation, the authors select a reasoning strategy based only on the problem
structure before generation. The result that a router using branching coefficients and topology features
outperforms post-hoc trace-based routers is compelling and provides a useful perspective for adaptive
reasoning.
Summary Of Weaknesses:
1. There appears to be an unexplained discrepancy between the aggregate accuracy in Table 1 and the
instance-level decomposition in Table 2. In Table 2, “CoT only” is defined as cases where CoT is correct and A* is
incorrect, while “A* only” is defined as the opposite. Therefore, if Tables 1 and 2 are based on the same evaluation
set, it would be natural to calculate CoT accuracy as “Both correct + CoT only” and A* accuracy as “Both correct +
A* only” for each dataset. However, for StrategyQA, if calculated based on Table 2, the CoT accuracy should be
approximately 73.8% and the A* accuracy approximately 70.3%, whereas Table 1 reports CoT as 64.52% and A* as
74.80%. In LogicQA and HotpotQA as well, the A* accuracy differs between the values calculated from Table 2 and
those reported in Table 1. If this discrepancy is due to different evaluation subsets, filtering, or aggregation
procedures, it should be clearly explained; otherwise, it could undermine the credibility of the paper’s core
quantitative results.
2. The term “topology” and the definition of the features are somewhat ambiguous. The topology features
proposed in the paper are closer to hand-crafted structural or input features—such as question length, context
length, logical connectives, negation markers, estimated hop count, distractor count, and number of answer
options—than to mathematical topology or TDA-style features. A clearer definition and justification are needed to
explain why these features should be considered to capture reasoning topology. Furthermore, the claim would
be more persuasive if the paper further verified that these features reflect actual reasoning structures rather
than dataset artifacts or surface statistics.
3. The rationale for choosing A* search and its relationship to other search methods are not sufficiently clear.
Although the paper explains that A* is used as a controlled diagnostic, the justification for why A* is more
appropriate for this research question than other search-based reasoning methods—such as beam search, Tree-
of-Thought, and MCTS-style search—is somewhat limited. In particular, since the paper’s conclusion extends to
the general question of when search is helpful, there should be a clearer discussion of whether the topology-
dependent behavior observed with A* would also hold for other search algorithms, or whether it depends on the
specific heuristic, frontier management, and cost function used by A*. The paper’s generalizability would be
strengthened if ablation studies with other search methods, or at least a more detailed comparative analysis,
were included.
Add:
4. The scope and role of human evaluation are limited. While the evaluation of some disagreement instances by
two annotators is meaningful as a supplementary analysis, it is somewhat limited in supporting the broader
claims about the relationship between trace fluency and correctness and the superiority of topology-aware
routing. Human validation involving more annotators, a larger sample size, and more diverse datasets would
improve the reliability of the analysis of the Fluency-Correctness Asymmetry and routing behavior.

Response to W2: Thank you for this observation. We agree that reasoning topology does not
refer to mathematical topology or topological data analysis. We use the term operationally to
describe the structural form of reasoning induced by an input: whether the problem can be
solved through a largely linear and globally coherent trajectory, or instead requires preserving,
comparing, or eliminating multiple partial reasoning paths before committing to an answer.
This interpretation follows the qualitative regimes identified in Section 3.2 and Appendix B. In
particular, 
 is most useful for non-linear evidence aggregation, hypothesis elimination, and
multi-hop composition, where several candidate facts, constraints, or reasoning paths must be
considered before commitment. In contrast, CoT is preferable for direct factual reasoning and
globally coherent argumentation, where a single continuous reasoning trajectory is sufficient.
We agree that the 13 proposed features are not topological invariants in the mathematical sense.
Rather, they are pre-generation reasoning-topology proxies intended to capture properties
associated with reasoning depth, branching opportunity, competing constraints, and the amount
of evidence that must be integrated. For example, the branching coefficient is defined as:
bc = (c_sent × hop) / q_len
where c_sent is the number of context sentences, hop is the estimated reasoning-hop count,
and q_len is the question length. This quantity combines contextual extent and estimated
reasoning depth while normalizing for question length. It is therefore intended as an operational
proxy for the amount of multi-hop branching structure afforded by an input, rather than as a
formal topological quantity.

clusters contains instances from multiple benchmarks, while the

Comment:
The rationale for choosing A* search and its relationship to other search
methods are not sufficiently clear. Although the paper explains that A* is
used as a controlled diagnostic, the justification for why A* is more
appropriate for this research question than other search-based
reasoning methods—such as beam search, Tree-of-Thought, and MCTS-
style search—is somewhat limited. In particular, since the paper’s
conclusion extends to the general question of when search is helpful,
there should be a clearer discussion of whether the topology-dependent
behavior observed with A* would also hold for other search algorithms,
or whether it depends on the specific heuristic, frontier management,
and cost function used by A*. The paper’s generalizability would be
strengthened if ablation studies with other search methods, or at least a
more detailed comparative analysis, were included.
Response to W3: We use A* not because it is always better than beam search, Tree-of-Thought,
or MCTS, but because it offers a clean way to study heuristic-guided search. Its design separates
path cost, heuristic estimate, frontier management, and branching. This makes it easier to
examine the main contrast with CoT: exploring and prioritizing multiple partial reasoning paths
instead of committing to a single linear trace.
Other search methods introduce additional design choices. Beam search uses fixed-width
pruning, Tree-of-Thought relies on an evaluator, and MCTS depends on rollout, value estimation,
exploration, and backup rules. These are important methods, but their extra components make it
harder to isolate why search helps.
We therefore do not claim that all search algorithms will show the same gains or the same
routing boundary. Our claim is more limited: problem structure determines whether there is
room for search to help, while the search algorithm determines how well that opportunity is
used. Problems involving multi-hop composition, hypothesis elimination, or competing evidence
paths are more likely to benefit from methods that delay commitment. However, A*, beam
search, Tree-of-Thought, and MCTS may differ in pruning errors, search efficiency, and
overthinking.
Our analysis thus has two levels. At the structural level, we study when maintaining multiple
partial reasoning paths is better than following one linear path. This idea is shared by several
search methods. At the algorithmic level, the methods differ in how they prune, score, explore,
and allocate budget. Therefore, we expect topology to matter across search methods, but not
